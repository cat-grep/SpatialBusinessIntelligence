Where are your best clients?

We mapped five years of records from a Southern California civil law firm to see who its most valuable clients are, where they live, and where the firm could look for more of them. Three findings stand out.

  1. 1

    A small group of clients brings in most of the revenue

    The top 8% and the other lucrative clients together are 42% of mapped clients and bring in 89% of revenue. The top 8% alone bring in a third.

  2. 2

    Ontario reaches far more residents than the other two offices

    Yet the share of lucrative clients is similar or higher around Los Angeles (44%) and San Diego (46%) than around Ontario (40%).

  3. 3

    The clearest room to grow is close to the offices

    Reach beyond 20 miles is about half what it is within 10. Several populous ZIP areas within 20 miles of Los Angeles and San Diego have few clients today.

Background

The firm is a civil law practice in Southern California with offices in Los Angeles, Ontario and San Diego. It keeps detailed records of every case, payment and billable hour, but those records say nothing on their own about where its clients come from.

This project adds geography to the firm's records. Using PostGIS and U.S. Census data, it maps where the most valuable clients live, compares each office's market, and estimates where the firm has the most room to grow.

Project aims

Six questions, one per chapter:

  1. Who are the firm's best clients?
  2. Where do they live?
  3. Where should the firm grow next?
  4. How has the client map changed since 2021?
  5. What makes a client valuable?
  6. Which revenue is at risk?

About the data

Firm records are anonymized: no names, and no map shows anything finer than a client point or ZIP area. Only clients with an address on file can be mapped, so the maps and every location-based number use the clients on the maps. Population and language come from the U.S. Census (American Community Survey 2018–2022).

1

Who are the firm's best clients?

Clients who pay well and don't need many hours to do it. Four in ten clients qualify, and they bring in nine dollars of every ten.

Revenue alone is a poor measure of a client. A $50,000 case that takes 800 hours earns less per hour than a $20,000 case finished in 60. So we score every mapped client on two things: net revenue (money in minus money out) and revenue per hour of attorney time.

A client in the top quarter on both is a Star. Top quarter on revenue only is High Value; top quarter on hourly yield only is Efficient. Everyone else is Standard. The first three together are the firm's lucrative clients.

How the tiers are calculated

NTILE(4) window functions rank all 1,071 mapped clients twice, once by net revenue and once by revenue per hour, inside the v_client_tier view. Every later analysis joins to this view. Clients with no recorded hours count as Standard unless their revenue alone is in the top quarter. The SQL is in the appendix.

2

Where do they live?

Mostly close to the three offices. The largest statistically significant cluster of valuable clients is around Ontario.

Eight in ten mapped clients live within 30 miles of an office

878 of the 1,071 mapped clients (82%) live within 30 miles of an office. The dashed rings mark that distance, and each ring is treated as that office's market.

The Los Angeles and Ontario rings overlap. There, each client and ZIP area counts toward the nearer office only, so no one is counted twice.

Los Angeles: fewer clients, larger cases

The LA market holds 358 clients with the highest average revenue of the three markets, $7,984 each, and the most Stars (35).

Half of them live within about 10 miles of the office. Measured against the 9.8 million people living within 30 miles, though, the firm reaches 3.7 clients per 100,000 residents.

Ontario: the firm's widest reach

The Ontario market has the most clients (424) from the smallest population (4.7 million). That works out to 9.0 clients per 100,000 residents, more than twice the reach of the other two offices.

Cases here are smaller, averaging $5,478. Clients are spread widely, from Pomona and Rancho Cucamonga through Fontana to San Bernardino.

San Diego: high client quality, low reach

Only 96 clients live in the San Diego market, but 45.8% of them are lucrative, the highest share of the three markets (on a small sample).

The gap in San Diego appears to be the number of clients rather than their quality.

Where the clusters are statistically significant

A hot spot test asks whether a ZIP area and its neighbors have more lucrative clients per resident than chance alone would give. 34 ZIP areas around Ontario pass at 99% confidence, running from Pomona and Chino to Fontana, Rialto and Jurupa Valley.

Los Angeles has 14 smaller hot spots, just south-west of the office. San Diego has none.

The blue cold spots, north of Glendale and around Anaheim, are the opposite: few lucrative clients for the number of people who live there. A cold spot can still have clients, and it doesn't mean the clients there are less valuable. It means the firm reaches few residents there. The large one north of Glendale is mostly mountains with few residents.

3

Where should the firm grow next?

Most likely within 20 miles of the Los Angeles and San Diego offices, in populous ZIP areas where the firm has few clients today. The widest gap is among Spanish-speaking residents near San Diego.

Reach falls with distance from an office

Within 10 miles of an office the firm reaches about 7 clients per 100,000 residents. Between 20 and 30 miles that drops to 3.1, less than half.

Clients from farther away are also less often lucrative: 48% within 5 miles against 38% at 20–30 miles. This suggests proximity to an office matters, although the data can't show why.

That gives us a fair benchmark for every ZIP area: how many clients would we expect here, given its population and its distance from an office?

Ten ZIP areas with the largest shortfall

For each ZIP area we compared the clients the firm actually has with the number its population and distance predict. Within 20 miles of an office, the shortfall adds up to about 197 clients around Los Angeles and 69 around San Diego, against only 18 around Ontario.

How has the client map changed since 2021?

New clients have shifted from the Los Angeles market toward Ontario. The typical distance between a new client and an office has changed little.

What makes a client valuable?

The type of work shows the clearest differences. Short, structured matters earn the most per hour, and the two largest acquisition channels bring clients of similar quality.

Exhibits 5.1, 5.2 and 5.4 use all 2,066 matters and 1,874 clients ($9.57M), including the 806 clients without an address. Exhibits 5.3 and 5.5 use mapped clients only, because tiers and distances need an address. Revenue per hour is total revenue divided by total hours for the group.

Referral clients live closer to earlier clients

For every new client we measured the distance to the nearest client who came to the firm earlier. Referral clients live closer to an existing client than Google clients do. That is consistent with word of mouth, though it may also reflect where referral clients tend to live. If it is word of mouth, past clients matter even when they don't come back (Chapter 6).

What the firm's own records can't tell us yet

  • 51.5% of revenue ($4.93M) sits in matters with no scope of representation recorded.
  • 63% of matters have no acquisition channel recorded, so the channel results above rest on about a third of the caseload.
  • 806 clients ($2.52M) have no address, so they can't appear on any map.

Filling these three fields at intake is the cheapest way to make the next analysis sharper.

6

Which revenue is at risk?

Probably less than it looks. Few clients ever return for a second matter, so a long silence is a weak sign of a lost client. Past clients may matter more as a source of referrals.

Method and limits

Data

PostgreSQL 18 with PostGIS 3.6. Four firm tables (contact, matter, transaction, activity) plus 2020 Census ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs). ZCTAs are the Census Bureau's stable version of ZIP codes. Population, language spoken at home and household income come from the American Community Survey 2018–2022 five-year estimates.

Spatial methods

  • Markets: each client and ZCTA is assigned to its nearest office by geodesic distance (ST_Distance on geography). A market is everything within 30 miles; where two offices' rings overlap, the nearer office takes the client or ZIP area.
  • Reach: clients per 100,000 residents per ZCTA, smoothed with global empirical Bayes so that small populations don't produce extreme rates.
  • Hot spots: Getis-Ord Gi* on smoothed lucrative clients per resident, with queen-contiguity neighbors, across the 640 ZCTAs within 60 miles of an office.
  • Missing clients: expected clients = ZCTA population × the firm-wide rate for its distance ring. The gap is expected minus actual.
  • Word of mouth: distance from each new client to the nearest client whose first matter came earlier.

Limits

  • The data begins in 2021, so clients from before then are counted in the 2021 group.
  • Straight-line distance stands in for travel time.
  • Hot-spot significance is not corrected for multiple testing across ZIP areas.
  • Correlations, such as between distance and client value, don't show cause.
  • Revenue per hour ignores differences in attorney cost.
  • Reach benchmarks assume every market could match Ontario. Competition and demand differ, so the dollar figures are for scale only.
  • One firm's records; the patterns may not hold for other firms.
  • Client records are anonymized. No map shows anything finer than a point or ZIP area.

Tools

PostGIS for joins, distances and views; QGIS for the market close-up maps (Exhibits 2.2–2.4); Python (GeoPandas, matplotlib) for smoothing, hot spots and the other maps; Chart.js for charts.

SQL appendix

The core queries behind each chapter.

Chapter 1 · Client tier view (v_client_tier) 
CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW public.v_client_tier AS
WITH client_revenue AS (
  SELECT client_id, SUM(funds_in - funds_out) AS net_revenue
  FROM transaction GROUP BY client_id
),
client_hours AS (
  SELECT m.client_id, SUM(a.hours) AS total_hours
  FROM activity a JOIN matter m ON a.matter_id = m.id
  GROUP BY m.client_id
)
SELECT
  c.id AS client_id, c.geom,
  COALESCE(r.net_revenue, 0) AS net_revenue,
  COALESCE(h.total_hours, 0) AS total_hours,
  ROUND((r.net_revenue / NULLIF(h.total_hours, 0))::numeric, 2) AS revenue_per_hour,
  CASE
    WHEN NTILE(4) OVER (ORDER BY r.net_revenue DESC NULLS LAST) = 1
     AND NTILE(4) OVER (ORDER BY r.net_revenue / NULLIF(h.total_hours, 0) DESC NULLS LAST) = 1 THEN 'Star'
    WHEN NTILE(4) OVER (ORDER BY r.net_revenue DESC NULLS LAST) = 1 THEN 'High Value'
    WHEN NTILE(4) OVER (ORDER BY r.net_revenue / NULLIF(h.total_hours, 0) DESC NULLS LAST) = 1 THEN 'Efficient'
    ELSE 'Standard'
  END AS client_tier
FROM contact c
LEFT JOIN client_revenue r ON c.id = r.client_id
LEFT JOIN client_hours   h ON c.id = h.client_id
WHERE c.isclient = true;
Chapter 2 · Nearest office and 30-mile markets 
-- office(name, geom): the three office points, kept out of this page
WITH nearest AS (
  SELECT ct.client_id, ct.client_tier, ct.net_revenue, o.name AS office,
         ST_Distance(ct.geom::geography, o.geom::geography) / 1609.34 AS miles
  FROM v_client_tier ct
  CROSS JOIN LATERAL (
    SELECT name, geom FROM office
    ORDER BY ct.geom::geography <-> geom::geography LIMIT 1
  ) o
)
SELECT office, COUNT(*) AS clients,
       ROUND(AVG((client_tier <> 'Standard')::int) * 100, 1) AS lucrative_pct,
       ROUND(SUM(net_revenue)::numeric) AS revenue
FROM nearest
WHERE miles <= 30
GROUP BY office;
Chapter 3 · Clients per ZCTA (input to reach, hot spots and missing clients) 
SELECT z.zcta5ce20,
       COUNT(ct.client_id) AS clients,
       COUNT(ct.client_id) FILTER (WHERE ct.client_tier <> 'Standard') AS lucrative_clients,
       COALESCE(SUM(ct.net_revenue), 0) AS revenue,
       z.geom
FROM zcta z
LEFT JOIN v_client_tier ct ON ST_Within(ct.geom, z.geom)
GROUP BY z.zcta5ce20, z.geom;
-- Joined to ACS population in Python, where the empirical Bayes
-- smoothing and Gi* statistics are also computed.
Chapter 4 · First-12-month revenue by intake year 
WITH first_matter AS (
  SELECT client_id, MIN(open_date) AS first_open
  FROM matter GROUP BY client_id
)
SELECT EXTRACT(YEAR FROM f.first_open)::int AS cohort,
       COUNT(DISTINCT f.client_id) AS clients,
       ROUND((SUM(t.funds_in - t.funds_out)
              / COUNT(DISTINCT f.client_id))::numeric) AS first_12m_revenue
FROM first_matter f
LEFT JOIN transaction t
  ON t.client_id = f.client_id
 AND t.date >= f.first_open
 AND t.date <  f.first_open + INTERVAL '12 months'
WHERE f.first_open <= DATE '2025-03-31'   -- only clients with a full year observed
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;
Chapter 5 · Revenue per hour by practice area (same pattern for retainer, channel, language) 
WITH matter_revenue AS (
  SELECT matter_id, SUM(funds_in - funds_out) AS net_revenue
  FROM transaction GROUP BY matter_id
),
matter_hours AS (
  SELECT matter_id, SUM(hours) AS total_hours
  FROM activity GROUP BY matter_id
)
SELECT COALESCE(TRIM(m.practice_area), 'Unspecified') AS practice_area,
       COUNT(DISTINCT m.client_id) AS clients,
       ROUND(SUM(COALESCE(r.net_revenue, 0))::numeric, 2) AS revenue,
       ROUND((SUM(COALESCE(r.net_revenue, 0))
              / NULLIF(SUM(COALESCE(h.total_hours, 0)), 0))::numeric, 2) AS revenue_per_hour
FROM matter m
LEFT JOIN matter_revenue r ON r.matter_id = m.id
LEFT JOIN matter_hours   h ON h.matter_id = m.id
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY revenue DESC;
Chapter 6 · Lucrative clients with no new matter in two years 
WITH last_matter AS (
  SELECT client_id, MAX(open_date) AS last_open, COUNT(*) AS matters
  FROM matter GROUP BY client_id
)
SELECT ct.client_id, ct.client_tier, ct.net_revenue, lm.last_open, lm.matters, ct.geom
FROM v_client_tier ct
JOIN last_matter lm ON lm.client_id = ct.client_id
WHERE ct.client_tier <> 'Standard'
  AND lm.last_open < DATE '2026-04-09' - 730   -- fixed snapshot, not CURRENT_DATE
ORDER BY ct.net_revenue DESC;
